\section{Introduction}
% Briefly describe the biological problem, the knows and UNKNOWS
The villin headpiece subdomain HP35  is one of the smallest proteins that folds autonomously, and one of the fastest. Its native fold is a compact three-helix bundle whose stability derives largely from a hydrophobic core formed by the aromatic residues \textit{Phe6}, \textit{Phe10} and \textit{Phe17}. The Lys24-Nle/Lys29-Nle double mutant (Fig.\ref{fig:hp35_structure}) studied here was experimentally found to fold in $\simeq 0.7\,\mathrm{\mu s}$ at $300\,\mathrm{K}$ with little temperature dependence  \cite{PDB}. Because of its short folding time, already in the early $2010$s it has been possible to achieve long unbiased all-atom simulations that resolve dozens of folding and unfolding events \cite{Lindorff-Larsen2011}, which made it  become a paradigmatic model system for protein folding studies.
A protein that folds this fast, over so low a barrier, is expected to populate only marginally stable intermediates that are difficult to resolve; yet a strict two-state description has never fully accounted for it. Both experimental \cite{Reiner2010} and simulation based studies \cite{Beauchamp2012, hierarchical_hp35, Sittel2018} have reported evidence of structural heterogeneity within its conformational ensemble. 
%DEFINING THE QUESTION

In this project, I analyse the well-known $305\,\mathrm{\mu s}$ trajectory of the Lys24-Nle/Lys29-Nle mutant published by Lindorff-Larsen \textit{et al.}~\cite{Lindorff-Larsen2011}. Thanks to the extensive sampling and the system's simplicity, the average folding time was estimated directly from the trajectory ($3.2 \pm 0.6\, \mathrm{\mu s}$~\cite{Piana2012}  - average waiting time in the unfolded state, which is $\times3$ the experimental folding time). I build Markov State Models (MSMs)~\cite{thebible} to ask whether HP35 possesses resolvable metastable substructure (e.g. off-pathway traps, intermediates, substates within the folded basin, or partially folded states). Building MSM and here a difficulty arises that is not merely technical. The MSM pipeline (feature selection, dimensionality reduction, clustering) has no unique optimal recipe, and different reasonable choices can emphasise different slow processes.  As a result, different pipelines may produce different sets of metastable states, with different populations. This motivates a deliberately redundant design: rather than committing to one pipeline, I build several and ask which conclusions survive across all of them and which are model dependent, if the description of the various model is compatible (i.e. at most one has more resolution and one less, but they need to agree at the coarsest level).

% DEFINING THE PLAN: TRANSLATING INTO THE LANGUAGE OF  THE COMPUTER

Concretely, four MSMs are constructed from two internal-coordinate representations (residue–residue contacts and backbone dihedral angles) each reduced by two methods, PCA and tICA. After spectral analysis and PCCA++ coarse-graining, the four models are compared on (i) the estimated folding timescale, (ii) the robustness of the resolved macrostates, and (iii) their structural interpretation.

Rather than the original all-atom trajectory of Lindorff-Larsen \textit{et al.}~\cite{Lindorff-Larsen2011}, I use the preprocessed version publicly distributed by Nagel \textit{et al.}~\cite{selecting_features}, which provides internal-coordinate representations selected and filtered specifically for MSM construction (see \cite{selecting_features} for feature definitions). Each time series contains $\simeq 1.5\times10^6$ frames at a sampling interval of $\simeq 200\,\mathrm{ps}$. I employ two of these representations as alternative feature sets: residue--residue contact distances (\texttt{hp35.mindists2}, 42 features) and backbone dihedral angles (\texttt{hp35.dihs.shifted}, 62 features). A third set of crystal-structure contact distances (\texttt{hp35.crystaldists}, 53 features) is used as an additional structural descriptor in later analysis. To enable structural visualization, I reconstructed the backbone atom coordinates from the raw dihedral-angle time series (\texttt{hp35.dihs}); all molecular renderings shown below are obtained this way.
\subsection{Methods for MSM construction}
All code is written in Python, using Deeptime \cite{deeptime} for MSM construction and validation and BioPython \cite{biopython} for analysis of the crystal structure (PDB 2F4K). A Markov state model discretizes the continuous high-dimensional dynamics into a finite set of states with transitions observed at a fixed time interval, aiming for a reduced representation that preserves the slow dynamical processes of the original system. The pipeline used here consists of the following steps:
\begin{enumerate}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{0.01em}
\item Feature selection (distances or dihedrals),
\item Dimensionality reduction (PCA or tICA),
\item Clustering of the reduced coordinates into discrete states (k-means),
\item Estimation of the transition matrix and selection of the Markov lag time $\tau_{MSM}$ (implied-timescale convergence test; see Supp. Methods \ref{sec:SI_methods} or \cite[Ch. 2.4.1]{thebible}),
\item Validation against the data (Chapman--Kolmogorov test; see \cite[Ch. 4.8]{thebible}).
\end{enumerate}
The first two steps are the ones explored most in this work. Together they compress the raw MD data into a low-dimensional space where clustering becomes feasible. Since k-means relies on Euclidean distance, rapidly interconverting conformations must map to nearby points in the reduced space; failing this leads to poor markovianity \cite{pyemma_tutorial}.

\vspace{0.2em}
\noindent\textit{Feature selection.}
Two feature sets are used: contact distances (\texttt{hp35.mindists2}) and backbone dihedrals (\texttt{hp35.dihs.shifted}). Following \cite{selecting_features}, a Gaussian low-pass filter ($\sigma = 2\,\mathrm{ns}$) is applied to each feature to suppress high-frequency recrossings between states.
%%%
\smallskip \newline \textit{Dimensionality reduction.}
Two linear methods are compared: PCA, which finds directions of maximal variance, and tICA, which finds directions of maximal autocorrelation at a lag time $\tau_{\mathrm{tICA}}$. PCA therefore captures the largest structural fluctuations, whereas tICA emphasizes the slowest dynamical processes: configurations far apart in tICA space tend to interconvert slowly, giving a closer correspondence between geometric and kinetic separation (see Supp. Methods \ref{subsec:SI_dimred}). Because MSMs aim to resolve slow kinetics, tICA is generally preferred. Combining the two feature sets with the two reduction methods gives four pipelines: \textit{tica-distances}, \textit{tica-dihedrals}, \textit{pca-distances}, and \textit{pca-dihedrals}.
Since HP35 folds on the microsecond timescale, $\tau_{\mathrm{tICA}}$ was inspected over $100$--$1000$ frames ($\simeq 20$--$200\,\mathrm{ns}$), well below the folding time, and fixed at $20\,\mathrm{ns}$ after checking that the leading tICA components were stable across this range. The number of retained components was chosen with an heuristic criterion so that the reduced coordinates can be expected to separate metastable states: relevant coordinates should show multimodal free-energy profiles and time series with long-lived plateaus separated by rapid jumps (Supp. Fig.~\ref{suppfig:dimred_figure}). This yielded 7 components for tica-dihedrals and 5 for the other three pipelines.
%%%
\smallskip \newline \textit{Clustering and lag-time selection.}
The reduced coordinates were clustered with k-means (k-means++ initialization, up to 100 iterations), testing $k = 30$, $50$, and $100$. The number of clusters and the lag time were chosen together, since they jointly control resolution and statistical reliability: more clusters provide higher resolution and speed up implied-timescale convergence, but leave fewer transitions per microstate and can disconnect the microstate network. The lag time must also stay well below the microsecond folding time \cite{Lindorff-Larsen2011}, or intermediate kinetic details are lost. Because the implied-timescale test alone does not fix the lag time, the final choice was a compromise between timescale convergence, network connectivity, and adequate sampling of transitions, and so remains partly qualitative. Transition matrices were then estimated by maximum likelihood with a sliding-window scheme \cite[Ch. 4.1]{thebible} enforcing detailed balance, retaining the largest connected component as the state space. From a grid search with $n_{clusters}=\{30, 50, 100\}$ clusters and lag times between $10$ and $100$ ns; $n_{clusters}=50$  and a lag time of $50\,ns$ is selected for all four pipelines, as this seemed the best compromise between resolution, markovianity and connectivity (Supp. Fig.~\ref{suppfig:timescales_test} and \ref{suppfig:largest_connected_component_vs_lag}).
\smallskip \newline \textit{Spectral analysis and coarse-graining.}
The number of macrostates is chosen from the implied-timescale spectrum, taking the largest gap between successive timescales as the number of resolved slow processes. Microstates are then lumped into macrostates with PCCA++ (robust Perron-cluster cluster analysis) \cite[Ch. 2.5.1--2.5.2]{thebible}. The algorithm uses the sign pattern of the dominant eigenvectors of the transition matrix to identify the slow probability-exchange modes that govern relaxation to equilibrium, and groups microstates accordingly (see Supp. Methods~\ref{sec:SI_methods}). The resulting macrostates are expected to correspond to the distinct metastable states of the system \cite{thebible}. PCCA++ returns for each microstate $i$ a vector of \textit{fuzzy memberships} $\chi_{ik} \in [0,1]$, the probability that $i$ belongs to macrostate $k$ (with $\sum_k \chi_{ik} = 1$); each frame inherits the memberships of its microstate.

\smallskip \newline \textit{Dynamical coring.}
Assigning each frame to its highest-membership macrostate produces a hard state trajectory, but near a state boundary rapid recrossings inflate the apparent number of transitions: these are more likely to reflect boundary noise than genuine interstate crossings. Dynamical coring \cite{dynamical_coring} is a heuristic procedure to suppress them: starting from the hard-assigned trajectory, a transition into a new macrostate is registered only once the trajectory remains there for at least a threshold time $t_{\min}$, so that only long-lived occupancies survive. In this work I applied coring to the discretized macrostate trajectories with $t_{\min}$ equal to one lag time ($t_{\min} = \tau_{\mathrm{MSM}} = 50\,\mathrm{ns}$).
%
%

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
\label{sec:methods_statistical_analysis}
\noindent\textit{Chapman--Kolmogorov test.}
Model self-consistency was assessed with the CK test as implemented in Deeptime \cite{deeptime}. The microstate MSM at $\tau_{MSM} = 50$\,ns was coarse-grained into four metastable sets with PCCA++; for a range of lag times $k\tau_{MSM}$, the coarse-grained transition probabilities predicted by the reference model (propagated to $k\tau_{MSM}$) were compared with those estimated independently at each lag. Predicted and independent curves track closely and relax to consistent stationary values (Supp. Fig.~\ref{suppfig:ck}, shown for pca-dihedrals; the others behave similarly).
%%%
\smallskip \newline\textit{State populations.}
Macrostate populations are estimated in two ways. From the trajectory, after dynamical coring (above), as the fraction of frames assigned to each state. From the coarse-grained MSM, using the soft memberships $\chi_{ik}$ as weights on the microstate stationary distribution, the probability of macrostate $S_k$ is computed as:
\begin{equation}
    P(S_k) = \sum_i \pi_i\,\chi_{ik}
\label{eq:fuzzy_assignments}
\end{equation}
where $\pi_i$ is the stationary probability of microstate $i$. The membership weighting down-weights boundary microstates that cannot be confidently assigned to a single set.
%%%
\smallskip\newline\textit{State lifetimes.}
Macrostate lifetimes are likewise estimated in two ways. From the cored trajectory, as the average residence time in each state. From the coarse-grained transition matrix $T$, as $\langle t_k\rangle = \tau_{MSM}/(1 - T_{kk})$, the mean residence time of a single-exponential process leaving state $k$.

\subsection{The four pipelines yield valid Markov models that already differ in their projected landscapes and timescales} 
\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width = \linewidth]{figures/msm_timescale_spectra_report.pdf}
\caption{\small \textit{\textbf{Relaxation timescales spectra} at selected lag time $\tau_{MSM}=50\,\mathrm{ns}$. The horizontal grey line marks the MSM resolution limit ($t=\tau_{MSM}$).}}
    \label{fig:timescales_spectra}
\end{figure}
%
%
%
Projecting each trajectory onto its first two reduced coordinates already shows that the pipelines describe the landscape differently (Fig.~\ref{fig:free_energy_surfaces}): the distance-based models yield a single dominant minimum, while both dihedral-based models resolve two, and the tICA projections show an additional well-separated basin. Very importantly, free energy surfaces do not generally have a simple interpretation in terms of dynamics (e.g. free energy maxima may not be kinetic barriers), therefore these surfaces are only indicative, but the differences already anticipate a pipeline dependence in the metastable structure resolved later.
At the selected common discretization ($n_{\mathrm{clusters}} = 50$, $\tau_{MSM} = 50\,\mathrm{ns}$; see Methods), the implied-timescale test (Supp. Fig.~\ref{suppfig:timescales_test}) shows that the slowest timescale is \textit{approximately} converged for all four pipelines and falls in the microsecond range, consistent with the known folding time of HP35. Two observations: first, the implied timescales depend far more strongly on the pipeline (features $+$ reduction method) than on the number of clusters. Second, the full relaxation spectra at $\tau_{MSM} = 50\,\mathrm{ns}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:timescales_spectra}) shows that
the PCA pipelines yield systematically lower timescales than the tICA ones, indicating that they approximate the underlying slow dynamics less faithfully \cite{NoeNuske2013}, as expected since PCA does not optimize for kinetic separation.
\newline
Taken together, these checks show that all four MSMs are approximately Markovian at $\tau_{MSM} = 50\,\mathrm{ns}, n_{clusters}=50$ and are expected to resolve the folding-unfolding transition as the slowest process, providing a valid common basis for the comparison that follows; at the same time, their timescale spectra already indicate that the pipelines disagree on how many slow processes exist beyond the dominant one.
%
%
\subsection{The dominant slow process relates to the folding--unfolding transition}
The dominant implied timescale is reasonably close across most pipelines (Fig.~\ref{fig:timescales_spectra}) and of the right order of magnitude ($\mu$s) to represent the folding--unfolding transition. A preliminary confirmation comes from the sign pattern of the first non-trivial right eigenvector, which closely follows the fraction of native contacts $Q$ (Supp. Fig.~\ref{suppfig:first_right_eigenvector}), suggesting that the slowest resolved mode separates folded from unfolded conformations.

The spectra differ, however, in what lies beyond that dominant process (Fig.~\ref{fig:timescales_spectra}). The PCA-distance model shows the largest gap between the first and second timescales, consistent with a strongly metastable, essentially two-state process. The other three show a less pronounced gap, and the tICA-dihedral model departs most from a two-state picture: its spectrum decays slowly, with several timescales remaining above the resolution limit ($t = \tau_{MSM}$) even though its slowest timescale matches the distance-based models.

To compare the models at the coarsest level, I impose a two-state PCCA++ decomposition on each and measure, for every model pair, the fraction of frames the two models place on opposite sides of the partition (the off-diagonal quadrants of Fig.~\ref{suppfig:fuzzy_memberships_models}). At this level the four pipelines are \textit{broadly} consistent: the two distance-based models disagree on only $\simeq 1.6\%$ of frames, and the distance/dihedral PCA pair on $\simeq 2.3\%$. The tICA-dihedral model is the most different, disagreeing with each of the others on $\simeq 8\%$ of frames.

For tICA-dihedral, the origin of this disagreement is visible in the single-model $S_1$ membership distributions (Supp. Fig.~\ref{suppfig:fuzzy_memberships_models}, top): sharply bimodal and near $\{0,1\}$ for the PCA-based representations, but broad and much fuzzier for tICA-dihedral. This fuzziness means the model does not resolve a clean boundary between the two basins—unsurprising, given that its spectrum lacks a clear eigenvalue gap. But the boundary sharpness is not the only issue: even the most agreeing model pairs retain a visible amount of probability mass in the off-diagonal corners (Supp. Fig.~\ref{suppfig:fuzzy_memberships_models}, top)—frames that \emph{both} models assign confidently, yet to opposite sides of the partition. This residual mass indicates that the four pipelines do not resolve exactly the same partition, beyond mere differences in boundary sharpness.

In summary, the pipelines agree on the coarsest description but not on its precise boundary, which was somewhat expected.

\subsection{The folded basin is mostly defined by the hydrophobic core}
\label{sec:consensus}
\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width = 0.4\linewidth]{figures/consensus_rmsd_violin.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width =0.5\linewidth]{figures/consensus_pymol.png}
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/consensus_important_contacts.png}
    \caption{\small \textbf{Structural analysis of $S_1$ consensus frames.}
    \textit{\textbf{Top left}: distribution of RMSD from the crystal
    structure (PDB 2F4K) for consensus frames (blue) and all other frames
    (orange); dashed lines mark the median and quartiles.
    \textbf{Top right}: overlay of eight randomly chosen consensus frames made with Pymol \cite{pymol}.
    \textbf{Bottom}: closest-heavy-atom distance distributions for the ten
    contacts that best discriminate the two frame sets, ranked by the score of
    Eq.~\ref{eq:importance_score} (most discriminating at the top). For each pair, the vertical tick marks the median of each
    distribution, and the black diamond marks the distance in the crystal
    structure.}}
    \label{fig:consensus_frames}
\end{figure}
The populations of $S_0$ and $S_1$ (Supp. Fig.~\ref{suppfig:fuzzy_memberships_models}) closely match the unfolded and folded populations reported by Piana \textit{et al.}~\cite{Piana2012}, which is consistent with $S_1$ being the folded basin. To characterize it structurally while avoiding contamination from boundary frames, I define \textit{consensus} microstates as those assigned to $S_1$ with high confidence ($\chi_{i,1} > 0.8$) by all four pipelines, and restrict the analysis to this subset ($\simeq 61\%$ of the trajectory).

A simple characterization follows from comparing, contact by contact, the distance distributions inside and outside the consensus set. For each contact present in the crystal structure (\texttt{hp35.crystaldists}), I score how strongly it distinguishes the two sets by the standardized median difference
{
\small
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:importance_score}
    \mathrm{score}_{i,j} = \frac{
    \mathrm{median}(r_{ij}^{\mathrm{other}})-\mathrm{median}(r_{ij}^{\mathrm{consensus}})}
    {\left[IQR_{ij}^{\mathrm{other}} + IQR_{ij}^{\mathrm{consensus}}\right]/2}.
\end{equation}
}
A large positive score indicates a contact that is markedly shorter in the consensus set—relative to the spread of its distribution—and therefore characteristic of the folded basin.

The highest-scoring contacts (Fig.~\ref{fig:consensus_frames}, bottom) are the long-range pair $(10,28)$--$(10,29)$, which packs helix~1 against helix~3, and the contacts among Phe6, Phe10 and Phe17 that form the hydrophobic core (Fig.~\ref{fig:hp35_structure}) and residues close by. Intra-helix contacts, by contrast, do not rank among the discriminating features. This indicates that appreciable helical structure persists in the unfolded ensemble, whereas hydrophobic-core formation is largely confined to the folded basin. The interpretation is corroborated by comparing all intra-helical contacts inside and outside the consensus set (Supp. Fig.~\ref{suppfig:twostate_helix}): outside, their distributions are much broader with systematically larger medians, yet still compatible with helical structure—especially for helix $3$.

Interestingly, within the consensus ensemble both the RMSD and the $(10,28)$--$(10,29)$ contacts are bimodal (Fig.~\ref{fig:consensus_frames}), which suggest the possibility that the folded basin contains two alternative conformations. This is investigated in Sec.~\ref{sec:dihedrals_substates}. 
% to conclude:

Taken together, these results indicate that the folded and unfolded ensembles are distinguished primarily by the hydrophobic core and the helix-1–helix-3 packing that encloses it, rather than by helix formation \textit{per se}.

\subsection{Two-state thermodynamics are robust across pipelines but folding times are systematically underestimated}
\label{sec:kinetic_estimates}
Interpreting $S_0$ and $S_1$ as the unfolded and folded basins, respectively, allows a rough estimate of the folding kinetics to be obtained. Although the resulting times should be regarded as indicative only, they provide a useful basis for comparing the different models. A more rigorous analysis would require computation of mean first-passage times and reactive fluxes on the full microstate MSM using transition path theory, which lies beyond the scope of the present work. To estimate lifetimes and populations from the trajectory, first I apply dynamical coring as explained in Methods. The resulting cored trajectory is shown in Supp. Fig.~\ref{suppfig:twostate_traj}. The folding time,
$t_{\mathrm{fold}}$, is then approximated as the average waiting time between successive unfolded-to-folded transitions in this cored trajectory. Results of folding times and relative populations are reported in Table~\ref{tab:pop_and_times}.
\newline The results are model dependent. Overall, trajectory-based estimates of the $\Delta G$ agree within one standard deviation with the original estimate of $-0.6 \pm 0.2\, kcal/\,mol^{-1}$ reported by \textit{Piana et al.} \cite{Piana2012} from direct analysis of the trajectory, while all models predict a shorter folding time (as the mean/median residence time in the state $S_0$) when compared to the value reported by \cite{Piana2012} ($3.2\pm0.6\,\mu$s).
Note that in principle another estimate can be derived directly from the PCCA coarse-grained transition matrix and fuzzy membership probabilities (see Suppl. Table \ref{supptab:pcca_model_estimates}).
\newline The thermodynamics are therefore robust across pipelines, whereas the kinetics are underestimated at this lag, most plausibly because the slowest timescale is not yet fully converged.

\subsection{Three-states PCCA++ on tICA models reveal a compact non-native state and a loop rearrangement}
\label{sec:tica_threestate}

The implied-timescale spectra of the tICA models (Fig.~\ref{fig:timescales_spectra}), however, show additional slow processes that remain above the resolution limit, motivating a finer decomposition. As a practical substitute I monitored the fuzzy memberships as $n$ was increased: for $n>3$ they became diffuse, with no microstate confidently assigned to a single macrostate, indicating an unreliable partition. This non-native topology makes $S_0$ a plausible off-pathway intermediate, i.e. a putative kinetic trap. Consistent with a rare but long-lived state, the cored macrostate trajectory shows a single visit lasting approximately $1\,\mu\mathrm{s}$.
\smallskip \newline
\textit{tICA-dihedrals.} Here the three-state decomposition splits the space into $S_0$ ($\simeq 6\%$), $S_1$ ($\simeq 25.8\%$) and $S_2$ ($\simeq 68.1\%$), with populations computed from the fuzzy memberships (Eq.~\ref{eq:fuzzy_assignments}). $S_1$ and $S_2$ recover the unfolded and folded/partially folded basins of the two-state analysis (there labelled $S_0$ and $S_1$, respectively), and $S_0$ is the new state. Its structural signature is less obvious than in the distance model. Comparing the dihedral distributions (Supp.~Fig.~\ref{suppfig:threestate_tica_dihedrals}), the clearest difference between $S_0$ and the other two states is confined to the Leu20--Pro21 loop joining helices~2 and~3 in the native fold: in $S_0$ the $\phi/\psi$ angles of Leu20 lie predominantly in the $\beta$ region, whereas in $S_1$ and $S_2$ they concentrate in $\alpha_R$. The $S_0 \leftrightarrow S_1$ transition therefore corresponds to a local rearrangement of this loop within the unfolded ensemble.

From the cored trajectory, $S_0$ has an empirical population near $3\%$ and a residence time of $0.32\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ (median; $0.52\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ mean). It is entered exclusively from $S_1$ and returns to $S_1$; no $S_1 \rightarrow S_0 \rightarrow S_2$ passages are observed. Were the loop rearrangement a common step toward folding, such passages would be expected, so their absence identifies $S_0$ as an excursion within the unfolded basin rather than a folding intermediate. Overall, despite corresponding to one of the slowest processes the tICA-dihedral model resolves beyond folding, this weakly populated loop state appears of little relevance for the folding mechanism itself.

The previous analysis (Section \ref{sec:consensus}) showed that several observables, including the RMSD and specific long-range contacts such as $10$--$29$, display bimodal distributions within the consensus folded ensemble. The same bimodality is visible in the free-energy projections obtained from the dihedral-based models (Fig. \ref{fig:free_energy_surfaces}) and is reflected in the distribution of the PCCA++ membership probability for macrostate $S_1$ in the two-state decomposition (Supp. Fig. \ref{suppfig:fuzzy_memberships_models}):
These observations suggest that the folded basin identified by the dihedral-based MSMs is itself heterogeneous and may contain two structurally distinct substates. But PCCA++, at least with $3$ states, did not identify finer structure within the folded basin. Here I move past PCCA++ indications and I "manually" partition the folded ensemble identified by the dihedral-based models according to the the peaks in the PCCA++ membership probabilities (see Supp. Figure \ref{suppfig:fuzzy_memberships_models}):
\[
\begin{cases}
\text{pca-dihedrals:}
&
\begin{aligned}
F_1 &: \{x_i : 0.75 < \chi_{i,1} < 0.9\},\\
F_2 &: \{x_i : 0.9 < \chi_{i,1}\},
\end{aligned}
\\[1em]
\text{tica-dihedrals:}
&
\begin{aligned}
F_1 &: \{x_i : 0.9 < \chi_{i,1} < 0.95\},\\
F_2 &: \{x_i : 0.95 < \chi_{i,1}\}.
\end{aligned}
\end{cases}
\]
The two subsets of microstates ($F_1, F_2$) are found to be distinguished primarily by the conformational state of a single residue, \textit{Asp3}. In one state, \textit{Asp3} predominantly occupies the $\alpha_R$ region of Ramachandran space, whereas in the other it preferentially populates the $\alpha_L$ region (Fig. \ref{fig:rama_asp3}, top). Furthermore, an analysis of contact importance, according to the same scored used before (Eq. \ref{eq:importance_score}), but taken in absolute value, indicates that the most important contacts are those involving the N terminus $Leu1$ (\ref{fig:rama_asp3}, bottom), and suggest the interpretation of these two substates as a "locked" and "unlocked" state ($F_1$ and $F_2$ respectively).

Both substates are individually metastable. From trajectory analysis (Supp.~Fig.~\ref{suppfig:f1f2_figure}, top), their residence-time distributions are close to single-exponential (Supp.~Fig.~\ref{suppfig:f1f2_figure}, bottom),  with a median residence time of $\simeq 0.4\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ (mean $\simeq 0.6\,\mu\mathrm{s}$; Supp.~Table~\ref{tab:f1_f2_dwell_times}), comparable to that of the loop macrostate that three-state PCCA++ \emph{does} resolve on tICA-dihedrals ($0.32\,\mu\mathrm{s}$; Sec.~\ref{sec:tica_threestate}). 
That PCCA++ resolves the loop state yet not these substates is not a contradiction but highlights the practical limitations of relying on spectral analysis for identification of metastable states. PCCA++ ranks slow exchange \emph{processes}, whereas a residence time measures the metastability of an \emph{individual} state, and the two need not coincide.

\section{Discussion and Conclusions}

% Briefly phrase your biological problem again.
This project set out to characterize the conformational ensemble of the HP35 Nle/Nle mutant from a structural standpoint. To limit the bias inherent in any single MSM pipeline, four models were built and compared, varying the feature set and the dimensionality-reduction step.

% Briefly summarise the results (max one paragraph). You do not repeat the words you used earlier, make it concise. Make it evident how your results answer the question you have asked.
% If possible, present a model. This can be a molecular 'mechanism', 'scenario', 'reason'. For example: 'A and B belong to the same family'; 'mutation XD likely changes the interaction in liver'; 'in disease T we have less phosphorylation sites or the level of phosphorylation decreased'; 'in this experiment, there is a problem with degradation of protein P. ' etc.– The model can be presented as: 'propose', 'suggest', 'indicate','hypothesize', or even 'support' or 'demonstrate'. If possible try to present a cartoon figure or scheme.
Taken together, the four models yield the following picture of the HP35 folding landscape:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A dominant free-energy barrier separates two metastable basins: an unfolded basin ($\simeq 31$--$32\%$) and a folded/partially folded basin ($\simeq 67$--$68\%$). The two are distinguished not by secondary-structure content but mainly by the presence or absence of the native hydrophobic-core contacts and the close packing of helices 1 and 3 (Sec.~\ref{sec:consensus});
    \item A lowly populated ($\simeq 1\%$), compact metastable state with a lifetime of $\sim 1\,\mu$s, in which helix 3 and the helix-2--helix-3 loop are folded but an extended, hairpin-like segment replaces helix 1 (Sec.~\ref{sec:tica_threestate});
    \item Two substates within the folded basin, $F_1$ and $F_2$, of comparable population ($33.8$--$35\%$ and $31$--$32\%$, respectively). They differ in the configuration of the N-terminus and are discriminated mainly by the $(\phi,\psi)$ angles of Asp3 ($\alpha_R$ in $F_1$, $\alpha_L$ in $F_2$) and by the presence ($F_1$) or absence ($F_2$) of several native contacts involving Leu1 (Sec.~\ref{sec:dihedrals_substates}).
\end{enumerate}
%Compare your results with something in the literature (use PubMed, or literature indicated in UniProt, PDB or any datasets we learnt about). 1-2 articles enough. The point is that you show how your results relate to previous ones. Different scenarios: 'support', 'in contrast', 'in addition', 'gives insights into'.

This picture agrees on several points with the macrostate model of Jain and Stock~\cite{hierarchical_hp35}, obtained from a MSM construction pipeline which used dihedral representation and coarse graining with most-probable-path clustering. Their intermediate $I_1$ corresponds closely to the substate $F_2$ found here. They also report an off-pathway, partially folded state $I_3$ in which an extended conformation replaces helix 1; this matches the putative kinetic trap resolved by my tICA--distances model, both structurally and in population ($1.49\%$ vs.\ $\simeq 1\%$ here). On these points the present analysis supports their description, and it further shows that $I_3$ is recoverable with a standard tICA/PCCA++ pipeline---here, specifically when the features are contacts rather than dihedrals.

On the methodological side, this project is a concrete illustration of how strongly the choice of MSM pipeline can shape the results,  even for a small protein and a high-quality dataset. Apart from the dominant two-state transition, on which all four pipelines agree, the finer structure they resolve differs markedly: the putative kinetic trap (Sec.~\ref{sec:tica_threestate}) was recovered only by the tICA--distances model, and the two folded-basin substates only by the two dihedral-based models (Sec.~\ref{sec:dihedrals_substates}). This is not suprising, as any single pipeline reduces the dynamics to one particular low-dimensional representation, the resulting models are therefore best read not as competing definitive descriptions but as complementary views of the same underlying dynamics.

A related point is the limitation of relying on PCCA++ alone for coarse-graining, especially when---as here---the implied-timescale spectrum shows no clear gap. PCCA++ failed to separate the two folded-basin substates even though their dwell time is comparable to that of the loop rearrangement it did resolve (Sec  \ref{sec:tica_threestate}, \ref{sec:dihedrals_substates}). This highlights that metastability (a long dwell time) and spectral slowness (what PCCA++ ranks) are related but not identical: a state can be long-lived without its interconversion being among the slowest processes, and such states are then easily missed by a purely spectral coarse-graining.

%\textcolor{orange}{ Slow doesnt necessarily mean functional. On the other hand, the hardly populated left-handed states identified by TICA are of little rel- evance for the folding process, although they account for the slowest time scales of the system. We note that the magnitude and convergence of the implied time scales of the resulting Markov model, which are commonly adopted as quality crite- rion for a dimensionality reduction method,77,81 naturally do not reveal this problem.}

Several possibilities remain for extending this analysis. The single most useful next step would be to test how robust the results are to the metastable-state identification procedure itself---for instance by comparing PCCA++ with alternatives such as MPP which was used in several MSM studies of HP35~\cite{hierarchical_hp35,  Sittel2018, selecting_features}. A more complete kinetic characterization would, in turn, require constructing the full microstate MSM and applying transition-path-theory (TPT) analysis.
